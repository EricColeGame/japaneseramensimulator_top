## How a six-player shift tends to be organized

The official description names the tasks but does not assign them to roles. Based on the advertised task list, a natural division of labor in a six-player session usually looks something like this. This is reasoning from the stated features, not a developer-prescribed system.

| Station | Typical focus | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prep | Chopping, portioning, organizing ingredients | Keeps the cooking station from stalling |
| Broth and noodles | Boiling, timing, assembling bowls | The throughput bottleneck in most ramen kitchens |
| Serving | Running finished bowls to tables | Prevents food sitting and customers waiting |
| Front of house | Greeting, seating, taking orders, checking on guests | Feeds the "looking after customers" loop |
| Cleaning | Dishes, counters, spills, clearing tables | Prevents the shop degrading over a shift |
| Floating | Filling gaps, restocking, covering breaks | Absorbs whatever is failing at that moment |

In practice, smaller groups will compress these roles. Two or three players can cover a shift by rotating between prep, cooking, and serving, with cleaning done during lulls. The floating role is the one most groups skip first and regret most, because it is the only role that reacts to problems instead of following a fixed station.

## Menu design as the real difficulty setting

The most consequential advertised feature is that players create their own menu. This is not a cosmetic choice. A menu defines how many ingredients you must keep on hand, how many cooking steps each order requires, and how much time a single bowl consumes at the cooking station.

Because the official page does not publish recipe quantities or prices, menu balance has to be learned in play. A reasonable approach, based on general kitchen logic rather than any documented game system:

Start with a small menu. Fewer items means fewer ingredients to prep, fewer stations to manage, and fewer ways for a six-player team to collide. Add variety only once the group can consistently serve the current menu without orders backing up.

Favor items that share ingredients. If two bowls use the same broth base or the same topping, prep work compounds instead of multiplying.

Watch which items slow the line. A dish that takes one player out of rotation for a long stretch is expensive even if it is popular.

This is player practice and general ramen-shop reasoning, not developer documentation. The game does not publish a formula, and any guide claiming exact optimal ratios is working outside the verified material.

## Camera, comfort, and cooperation

The official page notes that players can switch between first-person and third-person views, and that a view choice is available for players prone to motion sickness. That is a small but genuinely useful detail for a co-op game where one player may be sprinting between stations while another stands still at a pot. If movement in first person makes you uncomfortable, switch early rather than pushing through a shift, and mention it to your group so nobody assumes you are idle.

## Coordinating without built-in instructions

The advertised feature set implies constant verbal coordination: who is on broth, who is plating, who is clearing tables. The store page does not describe voice chat, ping systems, or role assignment tools, so plan to use whatever your group already uses outside the game. Agree on stations before the shift starts, and agree on who calls out when an order has been waiting too long.
