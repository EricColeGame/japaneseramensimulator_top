## What the developer says you will do

The Steam store description is the authority on the game's scope, and it is specific about the loop. Japanese Ramen Simulator is a ramen shop management simulator for up to six players. The advertised activities are:

- Preparing ingredients
- Cooking
- Serving food
- Looking after customers
- Cleaning up

Two structural features stand out. First, players create their own menu rather than picking from a fixed list. ## Co-op structure and role splitting

Six players is a lot for one counter. That number suggests the developers expect groups to divide labor rather than all do the same job. ## Menu design and recipe logic

Menu creation is an advertised feature. What the store page does not publish is a recipe list with exact ingredient quantities, prices, or a code system. Several things people commonly ask about simply are not documented officially. To be clear about the boundary:

**Documented:** you create your own menu. **Not documented:** specific recipes, ingredient amounts, costs, or any code-based lookup.

Third-party guides circulate for games in this genre, and some describe how players typically approach a ramen menu. Treated as player practice rather than developer documentation, the general logic is:

- Keep your opening menu short. A ramen shop's speed comes from repetition, not variety.
- Separate tasks by time cost. Broth and toppings have very different prep windows, and a menu that front-loads everything at once creates a bottleneck.
- Pair one slow item with fast items so the slow station never becomes the only thing happening.
- Add items only when your group has spare hands, not spare enthusiasm.

Those are general kitchen-management principles that apply to almost any cooking sim. They are not extracted from this game's data. Anyone claiming exact ratios or a recipe code list for Japanese Ramen Simulator is going beyond what the official page states, and you should treat such claims with suspicion.

## Shop layout and camera choice

Because you design the shop, layout is not cosmetic. In a first-person view, your sense of reach and clearance is tied to what is directly in front of you. In third-person, you get more spatial awareness but less immediacy at a station. Groups will likely find one view suits prep work and the other suits navigating a crowded floor.

The motion sickness option is worth taking seriously if you are sensitive to camera movement. Test both views before you commit to a long session, and use whichever setting the game offers for comfort rather than forcing the default.
