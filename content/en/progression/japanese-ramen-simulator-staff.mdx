Japanese Ramen Simulator is a co-operative ramen shop management game from developer Wild Dog, released on Steam on 9 October 2026. If you have seen the name floating around and assumed it was a solo cooking sim with a fixed recipe book, the official store description tells a different story: this is a team game for up to six players, and the kitchen is only part of the job. This article sticks to what the developer actually advertises, flags what comes from player reports rather than documentation, and is honest about the parts nobody has published yet.

## What the Developer Actually Advertises

According to the official Steam page (app 4968640), the game is a ramen shop management simulator for up to six players. The advertised activity loop covers preparing ingredients, cooking, serving food, looking after customers, and cleaning up. Beyond the kitchen work, players create their own menu, design the shop, and can switch between first-person and third-person views.

That last detail is worth noting. The store description specifically mentions that players can choose a view if they are prone to motion sickness, which suggests the developer expects people to move around the shop a lot rather than sit at a fixed station. You can read the full description yourself on the [official Japanese Ramen Simulator Steam page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4968640/).

There is no published recipe list with exact ingredient quantities, no price sheet, no shop upgrade costs, no staff system, and no code system. If a page claims otherwise, treat it as unverified.

## The Division of Labour in a Six-Player Kitchen

The advertised task list maps naturally onto roles, and this is where a co-op ramen game lives or dies. Preparing ingredients, cooking, serving, attending to customers, and cleaning are five distinct jobs, which lines up neatly with a small team.

| Advertised task | What it implies for a shift |
| --- | --- |
| Preparing ingredients | Someone works ahead of orders rather than reacting to them |
| Cooking | The timed, attention-locking job that cannot be abandoned mid-task |
| Serving food | The handoff between kitchen and customer, and a common bottleneck |
| Looking after customers | Seating, waiting, and keeping people from giving up |
| Cleaning up | A reset task that likely punishes teams who ignore it |

Because the developer lists cleaning alongside cooking, it is reasonable to plan for it as a recurring duty rather than an afterthought. That said, the store page does not publish penalties, timers, or failure conditions, so how harsh the cleanliness requirement actually is can only be confirmed by playing. If you are organising a group, decide who owns which task before the first shift instead of improvising, and rotate the least popular job rather than letting one player get stuck with it.

## Menu Design: What You Can and Cannot Plan In Advance

The developer confirms you create your own menu. It does not publish which dishes exist, what they cost, what they sell for, or how a recipe's ingredient list is structured in-game.

That leaves two honest options for planning:

**Option one — plan structurally.** Build a menu around a small number of shared base components so that one pot of broth or one batch of noodles feeds several dishes. This is standard kitchen logic and applies to any cooking game, but it is player practice, not documented fact.

**Option two — plan in-game.** Open the menu tools, look at what ingredients are available to you, and build outward from there. Whatever the game shows you is the authority; this article is not.

What you should not do is chase a specific recipe, ingredient quantity, or price you read somewhere. None of that is on the official page, and the store description gives no indication that such numbers are fixed across playthroughs.

## Views, Accessibility, and Setting Up Your Shop

Two advertised features are genuinely useful before you buy or host a session.

First, the camera. The game supports both first-person and third-person views, and the description explicitly frames this as an option for players prone to motion sickness. If someone in your group has had trouble with first-person games before, they have a documented alternative rather than a workaround.

Second, shop design. Players design the shop themselves, which means layout is a decision rather than a given. The developer has not published layout advice, recommended footprints, or upgrade rules, so there is no optimal build to copy. A defensible approach, based on the advertised task list, is to keep the path between cooking and serving short, since that is the handoff most likely to slow a busy shift. Treat that as reasoning, not a guarantee.

For co-op specifics — how lobbies work, how many players can join a given session in practice, whether progress is shared — the store page does not say. Check the official page and the in-game menus for the current answer.
