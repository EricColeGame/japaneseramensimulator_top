## The menu is the design problem, not a recipe list

The official material says players create their own menu. It does **not** publish a recipe list with exact ingredient quantities, prices or a code system. If you have seen a table of broth ratios or a bowl-pricing sheet attributed to the game, treat it as unverified. None of that appears in the verified description.

What that means practically is that menu design is a player-level decision rather than a lookup. Based on general cooking and menu logic, a sensible way to think about your own list:

- Keep the opening menu small. Fewer bowls means fewer ingredient types to prep, fewer stations competing for space, and a shorter path from order to counter.
- Group items by shared components. If two bowls use the same tare, stock or topping set, adding the second one costs far less prep time than adding something unrelated.
- Match the menu to your group size. With up to six players, a broader menu is more survivable because tasks can be divided; with two or three, a tight list is easier to control.
- Consider cleaning time as part of the cost. Every additional ingredient is another container, another surface, and another thing to reset.

This is player practice, not developer guidance. The game does not document exact quantities, so any specific ratio you read online is somebody's preference.

## Splitting work across up to six players

The six-player ceiling is the most consequential design fact in the description, because it changes what "efficient" means. In a solo kitchen game, efficiency usually means doing fewer things. In a shared kitchen, efficiency means not colliding.

A few general approaches that follow from the advertised task list:

- Treat preparation, cooking, and front-of-house as separate lanes when you have the headcount. Players crossing between stations is where bottlenecks and physical blocking tend to appear in close-quarters co-op kitchens.
- Assign cleaning deliberately. Because cleaning is named as part of the loop rather than an afterthought, leaving it unassigned tends to push work onto whoever finishes first.
- Stagger who takes orders versus who plates. The description separates "serving food" from "looking after customers," which suggests these can be handled by different people at the same time.
- Use the camera option as a personal setting, not a team one. Nothing in the official description ties the perspective to a mode or a host choice.

Because the store page does not describe how roles are assigned, whether there is a designated host, or whether progress is shared, those are questions to answer by playing with your group rather than by reading a summary.
