## The Core Co-op Loop, Step by Step

The advertised loop maps cleanly onto a real ramen shop's workflow, and understanding that flow is the best way to prepare before you ever load in.

| Stage | What it involves | Why it matters in co-op |
|---|---|---|
| Prep | Preparing ingredients before service | Front-loads work so the rush does not break you |
| Cook | Turning prepped ingredients into bowls | Timing overlaps with serving and cleaning |
| Serve | Getting food to customers | Orders stack up fast if nobody owns this role |
| Attend | Looking after customers | Keeps the room calm while others cook |
| Clean | Cleaning up | Neglect it and the workspace degrades |

The practical takeaway: with up to six players, the biggest risk is not difficulty — it is duplicated effort. In games like this, two people doing prep while nobody serves is a worse outcome than five people doing slightly imperfect jobs across every station. Decide who owns which stage before the first order lands.

## Designing Your Menu: What Is and Is Not Documented

The official page confirms you create your own menu but does not publish a recipe list, exact ingredient quantities, prices, or a code system. That gap matters, because a lot of search traffic around this game assumes a fixed recipe database exists. As of the verified store details, it does not appear in official documentation.

What we can say with confidence is the general logic any ramen menu follows, offered here as player practice rather than developer documentation:

- **Start narrow.** A small menu is easier to execute consistently than a broad one, especially while you are still learning the kitchen layout.
- **Balance your broth bases.** Different broths anchor different bowls, and a menu built around one or two bases keeps prep manageable.
- **Pair toppings to bases deliberately.** Toppings that suit multiple bowls reduce the number of separate prep tasks your team juggles.
- **Revisit your menu after a few sessions.** Once you know which orders overwhelm your station layout, trim or adjust accordingly.

None of that is a recipe. It is menu-design reasoning, and the specifics — which ingredients exist, how they combine, what they cost — are things you verify by playing or by watching the official page for updates.

## First-Person vs Third-Person: Choosing Your View

The store page explicitly supports switching between first-person and third-person cameras, and calls out that players prone to motion sickness can pick a view. That is a meaningful design choice rather than a cosmetic toggle.

First-person tends to suit players who want immersion and precise interaction with counters and stations. Third-person tends to suit players who need spatial awareness of a busy kitchen — useful when you are coordinating with five other people and need to see where everyone is. If motion sickness is a factor for you, the official page's own framing suggests testing both views early rather than assuming one is correct.

There is no documented hotkey or menu path for switching views in the official description. How you actually toggle it is something to confirm in-game.

## Playing Well With Six: Practical Advice

Co-op kitchen games live or die on communication, and six players is enough that chaos is the default state. A few habits help:

1. **Assign stations verbally.** Prep, cook, serve, attend, clean — name owners out loud.
2. **Call orders, do not assume.** In a loud kitchen, silence causes dropped tickets.
3. **Clean in the gaps.** Cleaning is advertised as part of the loop, so treat it as a task, not an afterthought.
4. **Pick your camera view per role.** A server may want third-person awareness; a prep cook may prefer first-person precision.

None of this guarantees success — it is structured reasoning about how the advertised systems fit together, not a walkthrough.
