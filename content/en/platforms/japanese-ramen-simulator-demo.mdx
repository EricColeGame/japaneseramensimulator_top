## The Six-Player Structure Changes How You Play

Most cooking games are solo power fantasies: you are one pair of hands against a growing order queue, and the tension comes from your own multitasking limits. Six-player co-op inverts that. The bottleneck stops being your hands and starts being coordination.

If the advertised task list is taken at face value, the natural division of labor looks like this:

| Station | Typical responsibility | Failure mode when ignored |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prep | Ingredients ready before orders arrive | Cooks stand idle waiting on prep |
| Cooking | Broth, noodles, toppings timed to order | Food sits and quality drops |
| Serving | Delivering completed bowls to the right customer | Customers wait, patience drains |
| Front of house | Seating, checking on guests | Tables sit dirty, new customers blocked |
| Cleaning | Dishes, surfaces, resetting tables | Throughput collapses late in a shift |

The important structural point is that cleaning is listed as a first-class advertised feature rather than an afterthought. In a six-player game, that usually means someone has to own it. Teams that treat cleaning as "whoever is free" tend to fall behind exactly when the shop gets busy.

## Designing Your Menu and Your Shop

Two advertised features give the game its management layer rather than pure cooking: you create your own menu, and you design the shop.

Because the developer has not published a recipe list with quantities or prices, there is no verified "best menu" to copy. What can be said safely is general restaurant logic, and it should be treated as player practice rather than documented mechanics:

- A smaller menu is easier to execute consistently with a rotating team.
- Dishes that share base ingredients reduce prep load across the whole menu.
- Dishes that share a cooking station compete for that station during peak orders.
- Anything requiring a long unattended simmer needs someone assigned to watch the clock.

The same logic applies to shop layout. A design that looks appealing in a screenshot may create a long walk between prep and pass, which costs you seconds on every single order. Test layouts during quiet periods rather than rebuilding mid-rush.

Developer-published material is the only source that cannot be contradicted later.

## Camera Choice Is a Real Accessibility Feature

The ability to switch between first-person and third-person is easy to skim past, but it matters for two reasons.

First, accessibility. Motion sickness in first-person games is common, and the store page explicitly acknowledges this by offering a view choice for players who are prone to it. For a co-op game you intend to play for hours with friends, that is not a minor checkbox.

Second, it changes information access. First-person gives you immersion and a tight view of your own station, but limited awareness of what is happening across the shop. Third-person gives you broader spatial awareness at the cost of some detail up close. A common practical split in co-op games with this option is for players on prep and cooking to favor first-person, while whoever is coordinating the floor favors third-person.

Treat that as reasoning, not a documented rule. The game does not publish recommended camera settings per role.
