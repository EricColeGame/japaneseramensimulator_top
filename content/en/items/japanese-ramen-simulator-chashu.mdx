## What the store page actually promises

According to the official Steam listing, Japanese Ramen Simulator is a ramen shop management simulator for up to six players. The advertised activity list is broad and deliberately mundane:

- Preparing ingredients
- Cooking
- Serving food
- Looking after customers
- Cleaning up

On top of that loop, the description says players create their own menu and design the shop. The camera can be switched between first-person and third-person, and the store page specifically notes that players prone to motion sickness can choose a view — a small but genuinely useful accessibility detail that many first-person kitchen games skip.

That is the whole verified feature set. Notably, the store page does **not** publish a recipe list with exact ingredient quantities, prices, or any kind of code system. If you have seen a "recipe code" chart circulating, it did not come from the developer's own store description.

## Why six-player co-op changes the way you cook

Most cooking games are a solo efficiency puzzle. Here, the interesting variable is division of labour. With up to six players and one shop to run, the bottleneck is rarely the noodles — it is coordination.

A practical mental model, and one that follows from the advertised tasks rather than from any official design document:

| Station type | Advertised activity | Why it matters in a group |
|---|---|---|
| Prep | Preparing ingredients | Feeds everything downstream; a stall here starves the pot |
| Heat | Cooking | Timing-sensitive, hard to abandon mid-task |
| Pass | Serving food | Connects kitchen to customers, exposes handoff mistakes |
| Floor | Looking after customers | Interrupt-driven; pulls people off fixed stations |

The point of the table is not to claim these are literal in-game stations. It is that the advertised task list naturally splits into fixed, timing-sensitive work and interrupt-driven work, and groups tend to fail when everyone gravitates to the same category. Players who take the moving jobs — serving and customer care — usually free the others to hold the cooking line.

## Menu design and shop layout: where the real decisions live

The two features with the most long-term consequence are the ones the store page mentions almost in passing: creating your own menu, and designing the shop.

Because no recipe quantities, ingredient prices, or costs are published officially, treat any specific number you read elsewhere as a third-party claim rather than documentation. Third-party guides have discussed menu and layout decisions, but they are secondary observations and should be read that way.

What is safe to reason about is the *logic* of a player-designed menu:

- A shorter menu concentrates prep and cooking into fewer, more repeatable actions, which suits a group that is still learning the shop.
- A longer menu gives each of up to six players a distinct job, which can help a coordinated team but punishes an uncoordinated one.
- Shop layout and menu size interact: a menu you cannot physically route through your own floor plan is a self-inflicted bottleneck.

For anyone unsure where to start, the low-risk move is to begin narrow, learn the shop's physical flow, then widen the menu once your group has a repeatable rhythm. That is player practice and general cooking logic, not a documented strategy.

## First-person, third-person, and motion sickness

The camera toggle is worth calling out because it is one of the few features the developer describes in concrete terms. You can switch between first-person and third-person, and the store page says you can choose a view if you are prone to motion sickness.

If you are sensitive to camera motion, third-person is the obvious first try. If you are not sure, the honest answer is that only you can test it — boot the game, switch views early, and adjust before you commit to a long co-op session. Nobody outside the developer can tell you which setting will feel comfortable for you.
