Japanese Ramen Simulator is a co-operative ramen shop management game from developer Wild Dog, released on Steam on 9 October 2026. If you have played restaurant simulators before, the pitch will feel familiar, but the specifics matter: this is a game built around a small team running one ramen counter together, not a solo tycoon game with a hundred employees to micromanage. ## Co-op Structure: Why Six Players Changes the Job

Six players in one ramen shop sounds like chaos, and in practice the interesting design question is how the five advertised activity areas divide up. Cleaning and customer care are the tasks that most single-player restaurant games push to the background. In a six-player co-op game, they become somebody's actual job during a rush.

A sensible way to think about the division of labor, based on the advertised activity list:

| Role focus | Advertised activity | Why it matters in a rush |
|---|---|---|
| Prep | Preparing ingredients | Sets the ceiling on how many bowls you can serve |
| Line cook | Cooking | The visible bottleneck during peak orders |
| Front of house | Serving food, looking after customers | Keeps seats turning over |
| Float / closer | Cleaning up | Prevents the shop from degrading over a session |

This table is a player-practice framework, not a developer-published role system. The store page does not describe fixed classes, assigned stations, or a staff system where you hire NPCs. Whether the game formalizes roles or leaves it entirely to player coordination is something you can only confirm by playing or by watching current footage.

## Menu Design: What "Create Your Own Menu" Actually Implies

The advertised feature is that players create their own menu. That is a meaningful design choice, because it means the game is not handing you a locked progression of unlockable dishes. Instead, the menu is something your group decides.

Community discussion of ramen games tends to obsess over exact recipe ratios. That is worth resisting here, because the official material publishes no ingredient quantities at all. What you can reason about safely is general cooking and menu logic:

- **Broth is the base layer.** In real ramen, the broth defines the style of the bowl, and toppings are adjustments on top of it.
- **Toppings and noodles are the flexible layer.** They are where variety and cost control usually live.
- **Fewer dishes, done consistently, usually beats a broad menu.** In any co-op cooking game, a menu your team can execute under pressure outperforms one that looks impressive on paper.

These are general principles, labelled as player practice — not documented mechanics. If the game surfaces specific ingredient quantities, costs, or a recipe system, that information will be visible in the game itself.
