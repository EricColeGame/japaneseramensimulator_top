## The Six-Player Co-op Loop

Six players is a large crew for a kitchen. The official description implies the work is split across the same broad stations you would expect in a real ramen shop: somebody prepping, somebody cooking, somebody serving, somebody handling customers, and somebody cleaning. A practical way to approach it: treat the first few sessions as a scouting run. Watch where bottlenecks form. In most co-op management games, the failure point is rarely cooking speed and almost always the handoff between stations, so having one person call orders and keep the counter clear tends to matter more than any single player being fast.

## Building the Menu and the Shop

The advertised feature that most separates Japanese Ramen Simulator from other co-op kitchen games is that players create their own menu and design the shop. This is a design layer, not just a service layer, and it changes how a group should think about a session.

Because the store page does not publish recipe quantities, prices, or a code system, recipe guidance has to stay at the level of general cooking and menu logic. Community practice in this genre tends to follow a few broad patterns, and these are player observations rather than developer documentation:

| Player practice | Reasoning |
| --- | --- |
| Start with a small menu | Fewer dishes means fewer ingredients to prep and fewer ways to confuse orders |
| Match menu size to crew size | A six-player lobby can handle more dishes than a two-player one |
| Group dishes by shared ingredients | Shared bases reduce prep work and storage pressure |
| Add complexity after the shop runs smoothly | Expanding too early tends to expose layout problems instead of fixing them |

On shop design, the same logic applies. Keep the path from prep to cooking to counter short, and keep cleaning supplies reachable without crossing the whole room. These are general principles, not documented rules, and the game may well reward layouts that break them.

For camera choice, the developer explicitly offers first-person and third-person views and calls out motion sickness as a reason to pick one. If you are unsure, start in third person, which tends to give better spatial awareness of the room and where your teammates are. Verify the available options in the game's settings menu rather than relying on secondhand descriptions.
