## What the game officially is

According to the Steam store description, Japanese Ramen Simulator is a ramen shop management simulator for up to six players. The advertised activity loop covers:

- Preparing ingredients
- Cooking
- Serving food
- Looking after customers
- Cleaning up

On top of that service loop, the store page states that players create their own menu and design the shop. It also confirms that you can switch between first-person and third-person views, and — notably — that players prone to motion sickness can choose a view accordingly.

That last detail is worth pausing on. It tells you the developer expects people to spend real time in a 3D space moving around a working kitchen, not just clicking through menus. It also means the camera is a designed feature rather than an afterthought, and you should pick your default view early rather than fighting it later.

## Building a menu that works for a team

The most interesting advertised feature is that you create your own menu. That is also the feature with the least official detail. The store page does not publish a recipe list with exact ingredient quantities, prices, or a code system, and this article will not invent any.

What can be said safely is general cooking and menu logic, framed as player practice rather than fact:

- **Fewer dishes usually means faster service.** A tight menu keeps ingredient prep predictable and reduces the number of transitions between stations.
- **Balance your stations.** If every dish on your menu needs the same pot, you create a queue. If your dishes spread across prep, cooking, and assembly, a six-player team can work in parallel.
- **Match the menu to your group size.** A two-player shop and a six-player shop have very different tolerances for complexity.
- **Iterate rather than optimize on day one.** Since the menu is yours, the sensible approach is to start simple, watch where service breaks down, then add variety once the basics are smooth.

Anyone claiming to know the "correct" recipe ratios for this game is going beyond what has been published. Verify in-game by testing a dish and observing how it plays out during service.

## Co-op roles and how to split the work

The official description lists five activity areas: ingredient prep, cooking, serving, customer care, and cleaning. With up to six players, that list maps naturally onto roles, though the game does not advertise a formal class or role system — this is a way to organize yourselves, not a mechanic the developer has documented.

A practical split for a full lobby:

1. **Prep** — keeps ingredients ready so the cook never waits.
2. **Cook** — owns the heat and timing, the hardest thing to hand off mid-task.
3. **Assembly and plating** — turns finished components into served bowls.
4. **Front of house** — seats customers, takes orders, manages the dining area.
5. **Runner** — moves finished food and handles the gap between kitchen and tables.
6. **Floater** — covers cleaning, dishes, and whatever is currently backing up.

In smaller groups, roles merge, and the floater slot is the first to go. The point is not the labels; it is that cleaning and customer care are advertised parts of the game, so a team that ignores them will feel the consequences.

## Camera choice and comfort

The store page explicitly addresses motion sickness, which is unusually considerate and worth taking seriously. If you are sensitive to camera movement in 3D spaces, switch views before you commit to a long session rather than after you feel unwell.

First-person tends to suit players doing close, precise work — cooking and assembly — because it puts you directly over the task. Third-person can make it easier to read your position in the shop and avoid bumping into teammates in a crowded kitchen. Neither is advertised as superior; the developer presents it as a preference, and you can switch.

## Designing your shop

Shop design is advertised but not detailed. The store page does not describe a tile system, a budget, or unlockable furniture, and no costs have been published. That means any layout advice is community practice, not documentation.

What generally holds true in any service-layout game: reduce travel distance between where food is made and where it is served, keep cleaning supplies near where mess accumulates, and avoid choke points that force teammates to pass through each other. Test a layout during a quiet period and change it before a rush.
